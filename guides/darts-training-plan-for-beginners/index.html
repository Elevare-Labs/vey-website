Darts Training Plan for Beginners: A Simple 3-Day Weekly Routine

Three focused sessions per week, around 30 minutes of structured practice each, plus a relaxed 10-minute warm-up. Build board control, scoring, doubles, finishing and match confidence with a routine you can repeat and measure.

If you're new to darts, it is easy to spend most of your practice time simply throwing at treble 20 or playing one 501 leg after another. That can be fun, but it makes it difficult to know what you are actually improving.

A simple training structure gives every session a purpose. This beginner darts training plan uses three sessions per week, each with around 30 minutes of structured practice, plus a relaxed 10-minute warm-up before you begin. The plan covers board control, scoring, doubles, finishing, bull accuracy and match-style play without making your training unnecessarily complicated.

Follow the same structure for four weeks and record a few key results along the way. The goal is not to become a completely different player in a month. It is to practice consistently and have something meaningful to compare at the end.

Beginner Darts Training Plan at a Glance

Day Main focus Structured training Total with warm-up
Day 1 Board control & scoring ~29 min ~39 min
Day 2 Finishing & doubles ~27 min ~37 min
Day 3 Mixed skills & match-style practice ~29 min ~39 min

A schedule such as Monday, Wednesday and Saturday works well for spreading the sessions across the week, but the exact days do not matter. Choose days that you can realistically stick to.

Start Every Session with a 10-Minute Warm-Up

The warm-up is not a test.

Throw freely for around 10 minutes. Do not count scores, track hits or turn the warm-up into another challenge.

Throw at different areas of the board, move between larger targets and simply allow your throwing motion to settle naturally. The purpose is to loosen up, find a comfortable rhythm and prepare for the structured session ahead.

You do not need to hit anything specific during these ten minutes. Once you feel comfortable, start the first exercise.

Day 1

Build Board Control and Scoring

Structured training: approximately 29 minutes.

The first session develops basic board accuracy before moving into more focused scoring and a short 301 leg.

Big Singles

Start with the numbers 1 through 20 in numerical order. Throw three darts at each number and count only darts that land in the large single segment. Trebles, doubles and misses do not count.

That gives you 20 targets × 3 darts = 60 darts. Record how many large singles you hit out of 60. This gives you a reason to use the entire board instead of immediately spending the whole session at T20.

Scoring Skill Check — 2 rounds

Throw 10 visits — 30 darts — at T20 and record your single 20 and treble 20 hits separately. Complete two rounds, giving you 60 darts in total.

Do not worry if the numbers are low at first. The useful part is having the same test available again later. Recording the two rounds separately can also show whether your accuracy remains stable as the exercise continues.

Score 250

Your target is simple: score at least 250 points in as few visits as possible. Play normal three-dart scoring visits and aim primarily at treble 20. Add every visit to your running total until you reach or pass 250, then record the number of visits you needed.

Finish with one 301 Single-Out Leg

Start at 301 and reduce your score using standard x01 scoring, but use single-out rules: you only need to reach exactly zero rather than finishing on a double. Try to complete the leg in as few darts as possible and record your total number of darts.

Single-out keeps the exercise accessible for beginners while still introducing the rhythm of an x01 leg: scoring, reducing a remaining total and eventually finishing the game.

Day 2

Practice Finishing and Doubles

Structured training: approximately 27 minutes.

Day 2 shifts the focus away from pure scoring. You will use more of the board, practice simple finishes and spend dedicated time on D16 and D20.

Around the Clock

Start at single 1 and work through every number up to single 20. You must hit the current large single before progressing to the next number. The objective is to complete all 20 targets using as few darts as possible.

This introduces target switching and forces you to adjust around the entire board. It is part of the structured session and comes after the separate 10-minute free warm-up.

Finish Practice: 21–40

Start at 21 and progress through every finish up to 40. You receive one visit — three darts — for each number and use single-out rules. If you finish the number within the visit, count it as a success, then move on whether you succeeded or not.

After reaching 40, record the total number of successful finishes out of 20.

Double 16 Challenge

Throw five visits — 15 darts — at D16. Every dart that lands in double 16 counts as one hit. Record your result out of 15.

Finish Ladder: 21–30

Start at 21 and try to finish it using single-out rules. Unlike the earlier finishing exercise, you are not limited to one visit. Once you finish 21, move to 22, then 23, continuing until you complete 30.

Record how many total visits you needed to complete the entire ladder. Your aim over time is to finish it more efficiently.

Double 20 Challenge

Finish the session with the same format used for D16: five visits, 15 darts, target D20. Record how many darts hit double 20.

Day 3

Put the Skills Together

Structured training: approximately 29 minutes.

The final session of the week combines board control, finishing, bull accuracy and short x01 legs. Rather than focusing heavily on one area, the goal is to move between different situations — closer to what happens when you actually play.

Big Singles

Repeat the Big Singles exercise from Day 1: three darts at every large single from 1 through 20. Record your hits out of 60 and compare how consistent your board control is across the week.

61+ Checkout Streak — 2 rounds

Start with a score of 61. You have a maximum of nine darts to bring the score exactly to zero using single-out rules. If you finish 61, move to 62, then 63, and so on. The first time you fail to complete a number within nine darts, the streak ends.

Your score is the number of consecutive finishes you completed. Play two rounds. This is more demanding than the 21–40 finishing practice from Day 2 and introduces larger remaining scores without requiring double-out finishing yet.

Bullseye Points Challenge

Aim exclusively at the bull for 10 visits — 30 darts. Score 1 point for an outer bull, 2 points for an inner bull and 0 for a miss. Add your total after all 30 darts.

Finish with two 301 Single-Out Legs

End the training week with two 301 single-out legs. Use the same rules as Day 1 and record the number of darts needed for each leg.

Try not to think about beating your personal best on every leg. Focus on sensible decisions and keeping your throwing routine consistent.

How to Use This Plan for 4 Weeks

Do not replace the exercises every few days just because one session felt good or bad. The value of a structured training plan comes partly from repeating comparable exercises.

Week 1

Learn the exercises and record your first baseline results.

Week 2

Repeat the same structure and focus on consistency.

Week 3

Look for small improvements in one or two metrics.

Week 4

Repeat your key benchmarks and compare them with Week 1.

One poor session does not mean the plan is failing, just as one personal best does not mean you have suddenly mastered a skill. Look for patterns across multiple sessions.

What Should You Track?

You can record every exercise if you enjoy detailed statistics, but beginners do not need a spreadsheet full of numbers. For a simple starting point, focus on four benchmarks:

Benchmark What to record
Scoring Skill Check Single 20 and T20 hits
Score 250 Visits needed
D16 & D20 Challenges Hits out of 15
301 Sprint Total darts

You can additionally track Big Singles hits, successful finishes, Bullseye Challenge points and your 61+ Checkout Streak. What matters most is recording the same type of result repeatedly.

What If You Only Have 20 Minutes?

A shorter focused session is still useful. Do not try to compress every exercise into twenty minutes. Keep the main focus of the day and remove some secondary drills instead.

You should also avoid skipping your warm-up completely. If necessary, shorten the free warm-up to around 5 minutes, then spend the remaining time on one or two focused exercises. On a scoring day, for example, you could complete the Scoring Skill Check and finish with a short 301 leg.

Common Beginner Practice Mistakes

Only throwing at treble 20

T20 matters, but scoring is only one part of darts. Beginners also benefit from whole-board accuracy, finishing and doubles.

Changing the routine every session

Variety can be enjoyable, but constantly changing exercises makes it difficult to compare results and recognise progress.

Practicing for a long time without a clear focus

More darts do not automatically mean better practice. A shorter session with a specific objective is easier to evaluate.

Ignoring doubles and finishing until later

You do not need advanced checkout knowledge as a beginner, but simple finishes and dedicated doubles practice belong in a balanced routine from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many days a week should a beginner practice darts?

Three structured sessions per week are a practical starting point for this plan. You do not need to practice every day to begin building a consistent routine. The best schedule is one you can realistically follow week after week.

How long should a beginner darts practice session be?

The structured sessions in this plan take approximately 27–29 minutes, with an additional 10-minute relaxed warm-up beforehand. Allow roughly 40 minutes in total for each session.

Should beginners practice doubles?

Yes. Beginners should not spend the entire session on doubles, but dedicated double practice should be part of a balanced routine. This plan uses simple 15-dart challenges at D16 and D20.

Should beginners practice 301 or 501?

Both can be useful, but this plan begins with 301 single-out. The shorter starting score creates finishing situations more often, while single-out prevents early training legs from becoming excessively long because of missed doubles. As your game develops, introduce more double-out practice and formats such as 501.

How long should I follow the same darts training plan?

Give a structured routine enough time to produce comparable results. Start with four weeks before making major changes, then compare your results and decide what deserves more focus next.
